What happens when an experienced practitioner keeps doing a task by hand while a newcomer learns to deliver it with an agent?

My hypothesis is that slow adoption can weaken the experienced person's advantage. If a substantial part of the entry barrier was the effort of producing an implementation, someone who delegates that work may become competitive before accumulating the same years of practice.

That is a hypothesis about changing competition, not a measured ranking of adoption across professions. Design, translation, and device software are cases to investigate. A convincing example in one does not establish replacement in all of them.

## 1. Separate the experience barrier into the work it contains

Consider a hypothetical team that needs an offline viewer for recorded device logs. It supplies a format specification, sample captures, and expected event sequences. The viewer must distinguish a real device fault from a missing or incomplete record.

An experienced engineer knows the device behavior well but writes the parser and interface manually. A newcomer has less device experience and uses an agent to build from the supplied material, then iterates against the expected sequences.

If the newcomer produces a useful viewer that passes the agreed checks, one route into this work has become more accessible. That says nothing about their ability to design the device or diagnose every fault.

The experienced engineer's knowledge still helps with ambiguous records and wrong assumptions. But if both people can deliver the required viewer, years spent mastering its implementation stack may no longer distinguish them as strongly on this assignment.

The [experience essay](/blog/what-is-experience-worth-when-ai-can-write-the-code) examines the knowledge needed to check delegated work. Here the question is whether declining to use the tool lets someone else become competitive sooner.

## 2. Waiting can change the comparison

Imagine the team needs a second view and then another supported format. The newcomer continues building with the agent and learning from each delivered result. The experienced engineer continues doing the same implementation work by hand.

If the agent-assisted approach keeps meeting requirements with less implementation effort, the newcomer has more room to try alternatives and take on work. They are also acquiring experience. Waiting can let someone else develop a working method and a record of accepted results while the incumbent relies on tenure.

Adoption alone doesn't settle the outcome. In our example, the newcomer might treat an incomplete log as a real fault. A polished display could hide that mistake until someone uses it to make a decision. If review and repair consume the apparent saving, the proposed advantage has not been established.

The comparison needs the same task, relevant quality checks, and the work required to reach acceptance. An impressive first demonstration is insufficient.

The same discipline applies to the other professions in my original hypothesis. A translation task needs its own quality criteria and qualified evaluation. A device task needs evidence appropriate to its hardware and operating constraints. These differences determine where competition can change; they are not reasons to assume either universal replacement or permanent immunity.

## 3. Career planning still has to make decisions

This creates a difficult planning problem. Someone choosing an education path or a specialization has to commit time before knowing exactly how the work will change.

Lex Fridman raises that tension in the DHH interview: focusing on present opportunities doesn't remove the need to plan education and a career while future tools and interfaces remain uncertain. That is a planning question, not a forecast that a particular role will disappear. [Publisher transcript](https://lexfridman.com/dhh-2-transcript/)

Learning to interpret a specification or investigate an unexpected result serves a different purpose from memorizing one agent application's controls. The controls may be useful now without carrying a whole career plan. A general foundation also needs contact with working problems, or it can leave someone unaware of what current tools permit.

For the person interested in device software, the log viewer could be a contained way to investigate that combination. Can they explain the format and failure cases? Can they use an agent to produce the viewer? Can they detect an implementation that gives the wrong answer? What parts still require help from someone with deeper domain knowledge?

Those observations can guide the next learning decision without guaranteeing future demand. A plan can keep a longer-term direction while revisiting its tools, rather than choosing or abandoning a field on the strength of one AI demonstration.

## 4. Test the adoption-lag hypothesis where it could be wrong

The experienced practitioner who combines domain knowledge with effective agent use may retain a substantial advantage. Early use of an unsuitable tool may also create rework rather than a lasting lead. Neither result should be explained away to protect the hypothesis.

Evidence for it would need to show less traditionally experienced people delivering comparable accepted work, with the incumbent's slower adoption contributing to the gap. Evidence against it could show that the difficult domain decisions or cost of validation dominate the work even after implementation becomes easier.

I don't think years of experience should be dismissed. I do think treating them as protection against a changing way of producing the result is a risky assumption. The question worth investigating is how much of that protection survives on the tasks people are actually being asked to do.
